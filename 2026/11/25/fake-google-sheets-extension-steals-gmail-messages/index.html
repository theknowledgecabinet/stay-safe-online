Fake Google Sheets extension steals Gmail messages

A piece of malware is installing a fake Google Sheets add-on in Chrome to read Gmail messages from Windows computers.

Wednesday 25 November 2026, 4.02pm

What happened

Researchers at Gen Digital have identified a new feature in malware called Remus Stealer. It silently installs a fake Google Sheets extension in Google Chrome on Windows computers. This add-on runs in the background and reads new emails from users who are already signed in to Gmail.

The extension sends the sender, subject, and body of messages to an attacker-controlled server. It checks for unread mail roughly once a minute. Because it reads mail from inside the browser, it does not need to steal your login cookies to work. Researchers found no evidence that it uses a security flaw in Gmail itself.

You can spot this fake add-on by checking your Chrome extensions page. Look for a Google Sheets card marked as 'unpacked' and a Developer Mode switch that you did not turn on. The fake extension borrows the description of the legitimate Google Docs Offline tool but has no spreadsheet interface.

If you change your password while the extension is still installed, it can keep reading your mail using your new login. This makes the theft look like it survived a password reset. The malware developers claim it works even after users revoke their sessions, but researchers say you must remove the software to stop it.

Am I affected?

The sources say this affects Windows users who have the Remus Stealer malware installed and are signed in to Gmail in Chrome. You can check your Chrome extensions page for a Google Sheets add-on marked as 'unpacked' or a Developer Mode switch you did not enable.

What to do

  • Check your Chrome extensions page for a Google Sheets add-on marked as 'unpacked' or a Developer Mode switch you did not turn on.
  • Isolate the affected computer from the network if you suspect an infection.
  • Revoke your Gmail sessions and change your passwords from a clean, safe device before signing back in.
  • Have the underlying malware infection investigated and removed by a professional or security tool.
