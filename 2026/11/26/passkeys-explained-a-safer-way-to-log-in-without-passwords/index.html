Passkeys explained: a safer way to log in without passwords

Passkeys have the potential to make traditional passwords obsolete, but there is still plenty of uncertainty surrounding them, including how they work and where they are stored.

Thursday 26 November 2026, 3.54pm

What happened

Passkeys are designed to make logging in simpler and safer. They use two linked digital keys. The private key stays securely on your device or in a password manager, while the public key is given to the website.

When you log in, the website asks your device to prove you have the correct passkey. You approve this using your fingerprint, Face ID, or PIN. Your device uses the private key to respond, but the key never leaves your device. The website checks the response against its public key to let you in.

You can create a passkey when a website asks you to, or by looking in your account security settings. Your device usually offers a place to save them. On Apple devices, they are in the Passwords app. On Android, they are in Google Password Manager. On Windows 11, they are in Microsoft Password Manager or Google Password Manager.

If you buy a new phone or computer, most managers sync your passkeys automatically. You should be able to access them on the new device once you sign in. Before erasing your old device, check that you can access your passkeys on the new one. Then, securely erase your data from the old device before selling or recycling it.

Am I affected?

The sources do not say who is specifically affected, as this is general advice for anyone using digital services. You can check if a website supports passkeys by looking for the option when you sign in or in your account security settings.

What to do

  • Look for an option to create a passkey in your account security settings for services you use often.
  • Check where your passkeys are saved, such as the Apple Passwords app or Google Password Manager.
  • Before replacing a device, ensure your passkeys sync to the new one and then securely erase the old device.
